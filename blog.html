Spend 2 Hours in the Library to Save 2 Weeks in the Lab

First published in 2026 · 6 min read

A very wise person told me this during one of my first few days as a PhD student at Virginia Tech. It came up during a group meeting:

“Spend two hours in the library to save two weeks in the lab.”

I understood what it meant, but I didn't really understand the wisdom behind it.

When I started my PhD, I loved being in the lab. Mixing chemicals, preparing samples, running instruments, and getting results was fun. I could easily get 10,000 steps a day just walking around the lab, and at the end of the day, I felt productive. I used to proudly say, “I can never sit at my desk all day reading and writing, but if you ask me to stay in the lab for 24 hours, I can do this all day!”

Looking back, I realize how naive I was - and maybe how much energy I had.

Now, five years into my PhD, I don't say that anymore. I don't want to spend my entire day in the lab. Maybe I'm getting old :|

But I think the real reason is that I've finally started to understand what that advice meant.

I now spend more time thinking about an experiment than actually doing it.

Somewhere along the way, I started to notice what makes me different from the first-year version of myself. It's not necessarily that I know how to pipette better, run an instrument faster, or work longer hours. I have started spending more time thinking about what I am going to do, why I am doing it, and how I am going to do it before I start.

These days, I try to have my experiment planned on paper before I walk into the lab. I write down the steps, calculate volumes, think about controls, consider what could go wrong, and - if possible - have a backup plan.

It takes time. But it also saves time.

And I've realized that being busy in the lab isn't necessarily the same as being productive. You can spend eight hours preparing samples, running experiments, troubleshooting, and cleaning up, only to realize that you didn't actually answer the question you started with.

Sometimes, the most productive thing you can do is not enter the lab yet.

Read another paper. Check a calculation. Talk to someone who has done it before. Think through the controls. Realize that your original experiment doesn't make sense.

Those two hours might save you two weeks.

I'm still learning this, but I think this may be one of the biggest lessons I've learned during my PhD (among many others):

Becoming a better researcher isn't always about doing more. Sometimes it's about thinking better before you do.

Five years later, I finally understand what that person meant.

Spend two hours thinking before you spend two weeks doing.

Learning to Think Like a Researcher

First published in 2026 · 4 min read

When I began my PhD, I thought research was mostly about finding answers. I expected the hard part to be learning the instruments, reading enough papers, and becoming technically skilled.

Over time, I learned that research often begins with sitting comfortably beside a question that does not have an obvious answer. The goal is not to make uncertainty disappear immediately. It is to make the uncertainty precise enough to investigate.

That shift changed how I read papers and plan experiments. I now pay closer attention to the assumptions behind a result, the controls that make it trustworthy, and the questions that remain open.

Being a researcher is less about always knowing what to do next and more about learning how to ask a better next question.

The Experiment Between Experiments

First published in 2026 · 3 min read

Not every useful part of research happens at the bench. Some of the most important work happens in the quiet spaces between experiments: cleaning up data, writing down what happened, and noticing a pattern that was easy to miss in the moment.

These pauses can feel unproductive because there is no instrument running and no new result to celebrate. But they are where observations become understanding.

I am learning to treat those pauses as part of the experiment itself. Good research needs motion, but it also needs enough stillness to make sense of where that motion is taking you.
